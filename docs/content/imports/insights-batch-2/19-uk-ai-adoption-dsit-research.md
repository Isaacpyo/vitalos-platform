If you only read the headlines about AI in British business, you would think every firm in the country had an AI strategy, a chief AI officer and a fleet of agents. The government's own research on UK AI adoption tells a very different story, and it is a far more useful one.

The **AI Adoption Research** report, commissioned by the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology and published on 28 January, surveyed 3,500 UK private-sector businesses. Its findings are sobering. Just **16%** of UK businesses use at least one AI technology. Another 5% plan to. And **80%** neither use AI nor plan to ([GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research/)).

But the number that should keep leaders up at night is not the adoption rate. It is what happens after adoption.

## The gap nobody is talking about

Among businesses that do use AI, **75%** report improved workforce productivity. That sounds like a win. Yet **77%** of adopters have seen no change in revenue ([GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research/)).

Read those two figures together. Most firms using AI are getting their people to do existing work faster. Very few are using it to sell more, serve new customers or build something the market will pay for.

That is not necessarily a failure. Time saved is real value. But it is a ceiling. Productivity gains on internal tasks are easy to start, hard to measure and easy for competitors to copy. Revenue growth is where advantage lives.

## Why firms get stuck at "faster"

The research hints at the reasons. Natural language processing and text generation is used by 85% of adopters, while agentic AI is used by just 7%. In other words, most AI in UK business today is a writing and summarising assistant. Useful, but rarely connected to the systems where money changes hands.

The barriers are telling too. The most commonly cited reasons for not adopting are a lack of identified need (71%) and limited AI skills (60%). Among the barriers rated most significant: ethical concerns, high costs and unclear regulation.

"Lack of identified need" is the interesting one. It rarely means there is no need. It usually means nobody has looked closely at where AI would change the economics of the business, rather than just the speed of the admin.

## Aim AI at the revenue line

If you want your AI investment to show up in the accounts rather than just the staff survey, change the question you start with. Instead of asking "where can AI save us time?", ask "where are we losing revenue we could win?"

That question leads to very different projects. Faster quote turnaround so fewer deals go cold. Better product data so customers can actually find what they want. Proactive service that spots a churning customer before they leave. Pricing that reacts to demand. Each of these touches customers and systems, not just documents, which is why they are harder. It is also why they are worth more.

A useful test for any proposed AI use case is to write down the business metric it should move and how you will see it move within a quarter. If the only answer is "people will feel more productive", it is probably an assistant, not a growth investment.

## Readiness is the real divide

There is one more figure worth noting. Among current adopters, 54% feel ready to scale their AI use. Among businesses still planning to adopt, only 34% do ([GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research/)).

Readiness, in our experience, is mostly about three unglamorous things: data you can trust, systems you can connect to, and someone accountable for the outcome. Firms that sort those out first tend to move from assistant to growth engine much faster than firms that start with the shiniest tool.

## A note for growth-stage companies

For scale-ups and growth-stage technology firms, the gap between productivity and revenue is especially sharp. Your investors are not paying for a slightly faster back office. They are paying for growth. AI that shortens your sales cycle, sharpens your product or opens a new market is worth ten times AI that drafts better meeting notes.

The UK does not have an AI awareness problem. It has an AI ambition problem. The firms that close the gap between "faster" and "bigger" over the next year will be the ones that pull away.

## Frequently asked questions

### What percentage of UK businesses use AI?

According to the DSIT-commissioned AI Adoption Research, 16% of UK businesses use at least one AI technology, 5% plan to, and 80% neither use nor plan to use AI.

### Does AI increase revenue for UK businesses?

The same research found that 75% of AI adopters report improved workforce productivity, but 77% have seen no change in revenue.

### What stops UK businesses adopting AI?

The most commonly cited barriers are a lack of identified need (71%) and limited AI skills or expertise (60%). Ethical concerns, high costs and unclear regulation were rated the most significant.
