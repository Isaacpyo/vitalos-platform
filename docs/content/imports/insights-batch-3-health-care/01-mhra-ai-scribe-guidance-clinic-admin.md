For fifteen months, anyone selling an AI scribe into the NHS had to assume it was a medical device. In April 2025, NHS England guidance said tools using generative AI to summarise consultations would likely qualify, and a follow-up notice said they should be regulated as at least Class I. Some developers went and got UKCA marks \[1\].

On 29 July 2026, the MHRA drew the line somewhere else \[1\].

## What the MHRA said

The test is intended purpose. Software is a medical device only if what it is for meets the legal definition of one. On that basis, these functions are not medical device functions on their own \[1\]:

- transcribing a clinical conversation
- summarising it: "summarization alone is not sufficient to trigger medical device requirements"
- turning information into structured data, such as problem or medication lists, for clinician review
- suggesting clinical codes for clinician review
- drafting discharge summaries or letters for a clinician to review and edit

## Where the line sits

Cross it and you are building a medical device. The MHRA's examples \[1\]:

- a summary with generated insights that suggest diagnoses, follow-up or treatment
- a summary-only tool whose maker claims it guides diagnosis and treatment planning
- an agent that finalises notes and places test orders without clinician review
- a report tool that suggests diagnoses from dictation and test results

Two things move a product across: what it does, and what its maker says it does. Marketing copy counts.

## What hasn't changed

NHS England has updated its guidance to match, but it isn't yet clear whether NHS procurement will stop asking for CE or UKCA marks \[1\]. The guidance gives no device class for products that do qualify. DTAC, the Data Security and Protection Toolkit and clinical safety standards still apply to anything deployed in the NHS.

## What clinics and suppliers should do now

- Write down the intended purpose: what the tool does, and what it must never do.
- Keep a person on every output. Review is what keeps drafting on the right side of the line.
- Read vendor claims closely. "Guides diagnosis" is a claim with consequences.
- Log what the tool suggested and what the person changed. That log is evidence for the next assessment.

This is good news for the most tedious work in a clinic. Letters, coding and inbox triage are where AI that drafts and suggests can save hours, provided it stays a drafting tool.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an AI scribe a medical device?

Not because it transcribes or summarises. It becomes one if its intended purpose is medical, such as suggesting diagnoses or treatment, or if it acts without clinician review \[1\].

### Can an AI tool suggest clinical codes?

Yes, for clinician review. The MHRA lists that among functions that don't by themselves make software a medical device \[1\].

### Does this change what NHS buyers ask for?

Not necessarily, yet. Some procurements may still ask for CE or UKCA marks, so check each buyer's requirements \[1\].
